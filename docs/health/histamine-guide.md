# Histamine intolerance — knowledge base

Last updated 10 November 2026. General information, not medical advice.

Evidence labels used below:
- **[Established]** consistent findings or broad clinical consensus
- **[Moderate]** supported by some studies, but small or mixed
- **[Emerging]** early research, mainly mechanisms or small samples
- **[Functional]** common in functional medicine; plausible but not well tested

## 1. What it is

- **[Established]** Histamine is a signalling molecule made by the body (immune cells, nerves, stomach) and also present in food, especially aged, fermented and stored foods.
- **[Established]** Histamine intolerance (HIT) describes symptoms that appear when histamine intake exceeds the body’s capacity to break it down. It is not an allergy: no IgE antibodies are involved, and it depends on dose.
- **[Established]** Dietary histamine is mainly broken down in the gut by the enzyme **diamine oxidase (DAO)**. Inside cells it is broken down by **histamine-N-methyltransferase (HNMT)**.
- **[Moderate]** The leading theory is reduced DAO activity, which can be inherited, caused by gut inflammation or damage, or caused by alcohol and some medications. It has not been proven in every case.
- **[Established]** There is no reliable lab test. Blood DAO tests and genetic tests do not reliably confirm or rule it out. The diagnosis rests on symptoms, ruling out other causes, and a response to a low-histamine diet with reintroduction.
- **[Moderate]** Prevalence is often quoted around 1% of the population, mostly adults, more often women. Numbers are uncertain.

## 2. Typical symptoms (often several at once, within minutes to hours after eating)

- Skin: flushing, itching, hives, **eczema flares**, swelling
- Gut: bloating, abdominal pain, diarrhoea, nausea
- Head: headache or migraine, dizziness
- Nose and airways: congestion, runny nose, sneezing, asthma-like symptoms
- Heart and circulation: palpitations, fast heart rate, low blood pressure
- Other: period pain, fatigue, poor sleep, anxiety-like feelings

**[Moderate]** Gut symptoms are the most common, especially bloating (Schnedl et al. 2019).

## 3. Rule these out first (with a doctor)

**[Established]** Similar symptoms can come from food allergy, mast cell activation syndrome (MCAS), coeliac disease, inflammatory bowel disease, SIBO, IBS/FODMAP sensitivity, lactose or fructose malabsorption, thyroid problems and medication side effects. Get a check-up if symptoms are severe, persistent or include warning signs (see the Health & safety notice).

**Get urgent help (112) for** throat or tongue swelling, difficulty breathing, fainting, or widespread hives with dizziness. That can be anaphylaxis, which is different from histamine intolerance.

## 4. The “histamine bucket”

**[Functional, useful model]** Think of tolerance as a bucket. Food histamine, histamine released by your own body (allergies, stress, hormones, poor sleep, infections) and anything that blocks DAO all add to it. Symptoms appear when it overflows. That’s why the same food is fine one day and not the next, and why lowering the total load helps more than avoiding one trigger.

## 5. Food rules

**[Established]** Histamine content depends on **freshness, storage and fermentation** more than on the food itself. Lists disagree, so use them as a starting point and test for yourself.

**Freshness rules**
- Eat meat and fish as fresh as possible. Fish frozen right after catch is usually low in histamine.
- **Leftovers build histamine:** cool fast and freeze portions right away instead of keeping them in the fridge for days.
- Avoid slow-cooked or long-held meat (bone broth, long stews) during elimination.
- Choose fresh dairy over aged.

**Usually high in histamine**
- Aged and hard cheeses (including raw-milk cheeses)
- Cured, smoked and processed meats (salami, chorizo, bacon, ham, sausages)
- Canned, smoked and pickled fish; fish sauce; shellfish that isn’t very fresh
- Fermented foods: sauerkraut, kimchi, kefir, long-fermented yogurt, kombucha, soy sauce, miso, tempeh
- Alcohol, especially red wine, champagne and beer
- Vinegar and pickles
- Tomato and tomato products, spinach, eggplant, avocado

**Often listed as “liberators”** (may trigger release of the body’s own histamine; **[Emerging]**, weak evidence)
- Citrus, strawberries, kiwi, papaya, pineapple, ripe banana
- Chocolate and cocoa, nuts (especially walnuts, cashews, peanuts)
- Raw egg white, some additives (benzoates, sulphites, MSG and colourings in some lists)

**May block DAO**
- **[Established]** Alcohol.
- **[Moderate]** Some medications, e.g. certain painkillers and antibiotics. Never stop medication on your own; ask your doctor or pharmacist.
- **[Emerging]** Black and green tea, mate and energy drinks are listed in some sources.

**Usually low in histamine** (when fresh)
- Fresh meat and poultry, fresh or flash-frozen fish, cooked eggs
- Rice, potatoes, oats, quinoa, millet
- Most fresh vegetables (except tomato, spinach, eggplant and avocado)
- Most fresh fruit (apples, pears, blueberries, mango, melon, peaches, apricots)
- Fresh milk, butter, cream, fresh cheeses (cream cheese, quark/kvarg). Cottage cheese is often fine
- Olive oil and animal fats; fresh herbs

## 6. Protocol (diet)

**[Established]** Standard approach (German/Swiss/Austrian guideline, Reese et al. 2017/2021):

1. **Low-histamine elimination, 2–4 weeks.** Fresh foods, following the rules above. Keep a symptom diary (the daily check-in).
2. **Reintroduction, about 6 weeks.** Bring foods back one group at a time, starting with medium-histamine foods and ending with the highest (aged cheese, ferments, wine). Note your reactions and portion sizes.
3. **Long-term diet based on your personal tolerance.** Avoid only what you need to, keep the diet as varied as possible, and manage the “bucket” (smaller portions, avoid stacking several high foods in one day).

**[Established]** Strict lifelong avoidance is not recommended. It narrows the diet and can cause nutrient gaps.

## 7. Supplements and medication (talk to a doctor or pharmacist first)

- **[Moderate]** **DAO enzyme supplements** taken 15–20 minutes before meals reduced symptoms in small trials (e.g. Schnedl et al. 2019; migraine: Izquierdo-Casas et al. 2019). They help some people around higher-histamine meals.
- **[Established]** **Antihistamines** (H1, sometimes combined with H2) are used by doctors for symptom control.
- **[Moderate]** **Vitamin C** helps break down histamine, and low levels are linked to higher blood histamine. Food sources are fine (but many vitamin-C fruits are liberators, so choose e.g. peppers, broccoli, apples).
- **[Moderate]** **Vitamin B6 and copper** are cofactors of DAO. Correct a deficiency if you have one; high doses aren’t proven to help.
- **[Emerging / Functional]** **Quercetin** and **luteolin** (plant flavonoids) may stabilise mast cells in lab studies; there are few human trials.
- **[Functional]** Some practitioners use zinc, magnesium, omega-3, *Perilla* or stinging nettle. The evidence is weak.

## 8. Gut and microbiome

- **[Emerging]** People with HIT may have a different gut microbiome, with more histamine-producing bacteria (Schink et al. 2018). Gut inflammation can reduce DAO, which is made in the gut lining.
- **[Functional]** Healing the gut (reducing inflammation, supporting the gut lining, rebalancing bacteria) is a core idea in functional medicine. It is plausible, but specific protocols are not well tested.
- **[Emerging]** **Probiotics differ.** Some strains make histamine (several *Lactobacillus* strains, including ***L. reuteri* ATCC PTA 6475**, which uses histamine as a signalling molecule; Thomas et al. 2012). Others are thought to be neutral or helpful (often cited: *Bifidobacterium infantis*, *B. longum*, *Lactobacillus rhamnosus* GG, *L. plantarum*). Strain-level evidence in HIT is limited.
- **Practical for the gut reset:** long-fermented L. reuteri yogurt, kefir and other ferments are high in histamine and may contain histamine-producing strains. In histamine-aware mode, start them later, smaller and slower, and watch for histamine-type symptoms.

## 9. Hormones, stress and sleep

- **[Moderate]** **Oestrogen and histamine influence each other**: oestrogen can increase histamine release and histamine can stimulate oestrogen production. Some women notice more symptoms around ovulation or before their period. Track your cycle in Forge to see your own pattern.
- **[Moderate]** Stress, poor sleep, intense exercise in heat, infections and allergy season (pollen) can add histamine from your own body, i.e. fill the bucket.
- **[Functional]** Methylation and HNMT gene variants are often discussed. Testing them is not clinically validated.

## 10. Skin and eczema

- **[Moderate]** Histamine drives itch. Some people with atopic eczema report flares after high-histamine foods, and studies have found lower DAO activity in some eczema patients. It doesn’t apply to everyone with eczema.
- Practical: track skin in the daily check-in alongside the histamine load. That shows whether high-histamine days are followed by flares for you.

## 11. How Forge uses this

- Foods get a histamine rating (low, medium, high, liberator, DAO blocker) based on common lists. Lists vary, and freshness matters more.
- Histamine-aware mode: the meal planner avoids high-histamine foods and liberators, the gut-reset tests put the high-histamine foods last, and the check-in includes histamine-type symptoms.
